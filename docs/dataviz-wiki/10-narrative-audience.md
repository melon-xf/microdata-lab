# 10 — Narrative & Audience

Sources: Knaflic ch. 1, 7–9 [K], Cairo ch. 1–3, 8 [C], Schwabish
ch. 3, conclusion [S].

## Explanatory vs exploratory

Decide FIRST [K doc 9; C pp. 15–16]. Exploratory = the reader hunts
(open 100 oysters); explanatory = you present the pearls. This
platform's analyses are **explanatory**: every chart ships with its
claim stated and its emphasis deliberate. Exploratory tools (the
explorer surface) stay neutral — no preattentive highlighting
[K doc 12].

## Before any chart: who / what / how

- **Who**: the specific audience and decision maker; their
  relationship to you; their literacy with the form [K doc 9].
- **What**: the action — "what do you need your audience to know or
  do?" Recommend explicitly [K doc 9].
- **How**: the data that makes the point — without hiding the data
  that doesn't [K doc 9].
- The 3-minute story: the whole argument in a paragraph. The Big Idea:
  one sentence with a point of view and stakes [K doc 9].

## Audience calibration

- The 5-second test: can a fresh reader say what the chart is for?
  [C p. 43; S p. 56]
- Don't dumb down: "graphics should not simplify messages. They should
  clarify them" [C p. 77]; underestimating readers = "stories for
  adults illustrated with graphics for kids" [C p. 61].
- But don't overload: ~4 chunks of working memory; layering and
  progressive disclosure [K doc 12; C pp. 78–79].
- Familiar forms beat novel ones for busy audiences; "bar charts should
  be leveraged because they are common" [K doc 10].
- Technical depth adapts: academic readers expect uncertainty shown;
  policymakers need the so-what first [S pp. 54–55, 393–394].

## Story structure (for multi-chart analyses)

- Beginning (plot/stakes) → middle (the evidence builds tension:
  "what is vs what could be") → end (the call to action) [K doc 15].
- Lead with the ending when trust is established or the audience is
  busy [K doc 15].
- Repetition: tell them what you'll tell them / tell them / tell them
  what you told them — transfers to long-term memory [K doc 15].
- **Horizontal logic**: the sequence of chart titles alone tells the
  story — an analysis's titles read in order must form the argument
  [K doc 15].
- **Vertical logic**: everything on one chart reinforces its title
  [K doc 15].
- Reverse-storyboarding: after building, reconstruct the story from
  the artifacts and compare with intent [K doc 15].
- One base order across linked views; emphasis moves via color
  [K doc 17].

## Honesty inside the story

- "What you show can be as important as what you hide" [C p. 25].
- The headline must match the data — K's pricing case: the draft
  headline claimed a decline the data didn't support; the headline
  changed, not the data [K doc 16].
- "If readers see only raw data, they will understand nothing" — but
  the filtering must be honest and declared [Rosling, C p. 257].
- Show your data; reduce clutter; integrate graphics with text;
  consider your audience — S's four closing principles [S pp. 393–394].

## Renderer/analyst checklist

- [ ] Mode declared (explanatory/exploratory)
- [ ] Each chart: claim sentence verified against the numbers
- [ ] Analysis: titles in order tell the story (horizontal logic)
- [ ] Linked views share base order and subject colors
- [ ] 5-second test on every frame (pixel QA + a fresh read)
